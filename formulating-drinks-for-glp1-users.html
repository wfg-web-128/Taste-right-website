What GLP-1 medications mean for drinks brands right now

Taste Right Technical Consulting

A significant share of UK adults now say they're interested in or already using GLP-1 weight-loss medications, and that shift is starting to show up in how people drink, not just what they eat. Appetite suppression changes portion size, sweetness tolerance, and how often someone reaches for a drink at all. For a category built on volume and repeat purchase, that's not a small thing to ignore.

Smaller portions, more deliberate purchases

People on these medications often report reduced appetite and a lower tolerance for very sweet or very large portions. A drink that leaned on a big hit of sugar or a large format to feel like good value may land differently with this growing group of consumers, who are choosing fewer, more considered products rather than grabbing whatever's cheapest per litre.

Protein and nutrient density are becoming a bigger deal

With appetite suppressed, getting enough protein and key nutrients from a smaller amount of food and drink becomes more of a priority for this consumer group. Protein drinks are already shifting away from a purely sports-performance image toward a broader, more everyday wellness positioning, and that shift lines up closely with what GLP-1 users are actually looking for. For the broader principles behind building a drink like this, see how to develop a functional drink that actually works.

Explicit weight-loss claims are still a grey area

Despite clear demand, most new drink launches are still cautious about making direct weight-management claims, partly for regulatory reasons and partly because the space is new enough that nobody wants to overreach. That caution leaves room for brands who can talk about satiety, nutrient density, or portion control credibly and legally, without making claims they can't back up.

What this means for formulation decisions

  • Lower, more considered sweetness levels rather than maximum sugar or sweetener intensity
  • Smaller, more concentrated formats over large, dilute ones
  • A genuine functional or nutritional benefit that holds up to scrutiny, not just a label claim
  • Messaging built around how the product fits into someone's day, not just what's in it

This is still an early, fast-moving shift, and it won't be right for every brand to chase. But if your target consumer overlaps with the growing group managing their weight through medication, it's worth factoring into recipe and format decisions now, rather than reformulating in eighteen months once the market's caught up. It's part of a wider shift in what UK consumers are choosing to drink, alongside the continued rise of low and no-alcohol drinks.

Common questions

Should my drink make a weight-loss claim to target this market?

Be cautious. Explicit weight-management claims sit in a complex regulatory space in the UK, and most brands are better served focusing on genuine, provable benefits like protein content, low sweetness, or nutrient density rather than direct weight-loss messaging.

Does this trend only affect diet or health drinks?

No. It's already influencing format size, sweetness levels, and protein content across a much wider range of categories, since a meaningful and growing share of consumers are simply drinking and eating less overall.

Is this a passing trend or worth building a recipe around now?

It's moving fast enough that waiting to see what happens carries its own risk. If your target consumer overlaps with this group, factoring it into formulation now is more efficient than reformulating a finished product later.
