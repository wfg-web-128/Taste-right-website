Launching a low or no-alcohol drink in the UK: what's actually changed

Taste Right Technical Consulting

Low and no-alcohol drinks stopped being a niche a while ago. The majority of UK drinkers now say they regularly choose a low or no-alcohol option, and sales keep climbing across beer, wine, and spirits alternatives. For a founder thinking about launching into this space, the opportunity is real, but the category has also matured fast enough that the bar for a first product is much higher than it was even a couple of years ago. It's one of several shifts in UK drinking habits worth watching right now, alongside what GLP-1 medications mean for drinks brands.

The novelty phase is over

Early low and no-alcohol drinks got a pass on flavour because the format itself was the point of difference. That's no longer true. Consumers who tried the first wave out of curiosity are now either repeat buyers of the brands that actually tasted good, or they've written the category off because the product they tried didn't deliver. A single alcohol-free line isn't a strategy anymore, it needs real flavour complexity and honest branding to compete.

Who's actually buying, and why it matters for formulation

Younger drinkers are leading the shift, but interest spans a wide age range, and premium, higher-quality products are growing fastest, not the value end of the category. That has real implications for formulation: cutting corners on flavour to hit a lower price point is increasingly the wrong bet. Consumers in this category are often willing to pay close to, or the same as, the alcoholic equivalent for something that actually delivers.

Alcohol removal changes more than the ABV

Alcohol carries flavour, mouthfeel, and preservation properties that most recipes lean on without realising it until they're gone. A low or no-alcohol reformulation of an existing recipe, or a from-scratch functional non-alcoholic drink, needs its own stability and shelf-life testing, not a straight assumption that removing the alcohol is the only thing that changes.

Positioning beyond "instead of a drink"

The strongest brands in this space aren't just selling an alcohol substitute, they're building genuine occasions and identities around the product, whether that's a sophisticated alternative for a night out or a functional, better-for-you everyday drink. Deciding early which of those two lanes your brand actually sits in shapes almost every other decision, from packaging format to where you'll get your first listings.

What to get right before you launch

  • A recipe that's been properly reformulated and stability-tested without alcohol, not just diluted
  • Clear positioning: are you a credible alternative to an alcoholic drink, or a standalone functional product?
  • Premium execution on flavour and packaging, since the value end of this category is the most crowded and least differentiated
  • A co-packer genuinely experienced with low and no-alcohol production, since the process isn't identical to a standard soft drink line

Finding that specialist co-packer is its own process, see how to find the right co-packer in London and the UK for what to look for.

Common questions

Is the low and no-alcohol market still growing in the UK?

Yes, strongly. The majority of UK drinkers now say they regularly choose low or no-alcohol options, with premium products in particular growing faster than value-priced ones, which is a shift worth building a launch strategy around.

Can I just remove the alcohol from an existing recipe?

Not without proper reformulation. Alcohol affects flavour, mouthfeel, and shelf stability in ways that aren't obvious until it's removed, so a low or no-alcohol version usually needs its own recipe development and stability testing, not a simple substitution.

Do I need a specialist co-packer for low or no-alcohol drinks?

It helps significantly. The production process for de-alcoholised or alcohol-free drinks isn't identical to a standard soft drink line, so a co-packer with genuine experience in the category reduces the risk of stability or quality issues down the line.
